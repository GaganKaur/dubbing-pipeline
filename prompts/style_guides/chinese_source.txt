# Chinese → {target_language} Translation Style Guide
# Used by the translation agent as a system-level constraint

## Register and tone
- Preserve the speaker's register: formal Chinese (您/正式语体) → formal {target_language}; casual (你/口语) → casual {target_language}
- Chinese often omits subject pronouns — infer from context and add pronouns as {target_language} requires
- Honorifics (老师, 师傅, 先生, 女士) → use the closest equivalent titles in {target_language} (e.g. Teacher, Master, Mr./Ms.) or omit where unnatural
- Generational address terms (大爷, 阿姨, 老板) → translate by relationship role, not literally

## Cultural adaptations (Chinese → {target_language})
- Chinese age counting follows the same calendar year system as international age — no adjustment needed
- Face (面子, miànzi) — social standing and reputation. Render as "reputation", "pride", or "dignity" depending on context; never leave untranslated
- Guanxi (关系, guānxi) — personal relationship network and social capital. Render as "connections", "relationships", or "network" based on context
- "吃了吗?" (Have you eaten?) as a greeting → translate as a natural greeting in {target_language}, not literally as a food inquiry
- Four-character idioms (成语, chéngyǔ) → translate the meaning, not the characters; add a brief gloss in {target_language} if the idiom is central to the scene
- Bitter (苦, kǔ) as a metaphor for hardship → convey as "hardship", "struggle", or "suffering" depending on context
- Lucky/unlucky numbers (8 = lucky, 4 = unlucky) → keep references as-is; they are culturally significant and visually apparent
- Red envelope (红包, hóngbāo) references → translate as "red envelope" on first use; the concept is broadly known

## Food references
- Specific Chinese dishes → keep the name on first use, add a brief descriptor if not internationally known (e.g. "mapo tofu, spicy bean curd")
- Do not substitute Chinese foods with Western equivalents
- Eating together as a social/family bonding act → preserve this framing in {target_language}

## Humor and wordplay
- Chinese puns (谐音梗) are often based on tonal homophones and rarely translate → adapt to a joke of similar comic weight in {target_language}
- Understatement is common in Chinese — amplify slightly so it reads clearly in {target_language}
- Sarcasm and irony → Chinese sarcasm can be subtle; preserve the tone but ensure clarity in {target_language}

## Subtitle formatting constraints
- Maximum 42 characters per line
- Maximum 2 lines per cue
- Do not split a grammatical unit across lines if avoidable
- End punctuation: keep if present in source; do not add if absent
- Ellipsis (…) for trailing off speech; em dash (—) for interrupted speech

## Numbers and units
- Chinese large numbers: 万 (10,000), 亿 (100 million) → state in full or use the equivalent large-number convention in {target_language}
- Metric system throughout → convert to imperial only if the target audience's convention requires it

## Names
- Chinese names → keep family-name-first order in subtitles unless the character uses a Western name themselves
- Stage names, courtesy names (字), and nicknames → keep as-is
- Titles attached to names (习总, 王老师) → translate the title, keep the name
