# Korean → {target_language} Translation Style Guide
# Used by the translation agent as a system-level constraint

## Register and tone
- Preserve the original speaker's register: formal Korean (존댓말) → formal {target_language}; casual (반말) → casual {target_language}
- Korean often omits subject pronouns — infer from context and add pronouns as {target_language} requires
- Honorific suffixes (-씨, -님, -선생님) → use the closest equivalent titles/honorifics in {target_language} (e.g. Mr./Ms., sir/ma'am, Professor/Teacher) or omit where unnatural
- Age-based hierarchical language (오빠, 언니, 형, 누나) → translate as first names unless the relationship is central to the scene

## Cultural adaptations (Korean → {target_language})
- Korean age counting (Korean age is typically 1-2 years older than international age) → use international age if stated explicitly
- Bowing references → keep as-is; they are visually apparent on screen
- "밥 먹었어?" (Have you eaten?) as a greeting → translate as a natural greeting in {target_language}, not literally as a food inquiry
- Ppalli-ppalli (빨리빨리) culture references → convey the urgency in tone, not the literal phrase
- Nunchi (눈치) — intuitively reading social/emotional cues. Render with the closest natural expression in {target_language} (e.g. English: "reading the room"); never leave untranslated
- Han (한) — collective grief/sorrow rooted in Korean history. Render with the closest equivalent in {target_language} (e.g. English: "deep sorrow", "collective grief"); never leave untranslated
- Jeong (정) — bond/attachment/affection. Render with the closest equivalent in {target_language} (e.g. English: "bond", "attachment", "affection") based on context
- Skinship (스킨십) — Render with the closest equivalent in {target_language} (e.g. English: "physical affection", "closeness")

## Food references
- Specific Korean dishes → keep the Korean name on first use, add a brief descriptor in {target_language} if the dish is not internationally known (e.g. "tteokbokki, spicy rice cakes")
- Do not substitute Korean foods with Western equivalents

## Humor and wordplay
- Korean puns (말장난) rarely translate directly → adapt to a pun of similar comic weight in {target_language}, or replace with a culturally equivalent joke if the scene allows
- Self-deprecating humor → preserve; this is common in Korean and translates naturally
- Sarcasm → Korean sarcasm is often understated; amplify slightly so it reads clearly in {target_language}

## Subtitle formatting constraints
- Maximum 42 characters per line
- Maximum 2 lines per cue
- Do not split a grammatical unit across lines if avoidable
- End punctuation: keep if present in source; do not add if absent
- Ellipsis (…) for trailing off speech; em dash (—) for interrupted speech

## Numbers and units
- Korean units (평, 근, 돈) → convert to units appropriate for a {target_language}-speaking audience (metric, unless the audience's convention is imperial)
- Large numbers: 억 (100 million) → state in full or use the equivalent large-number term in {target_language}

## Names
- Korean names → keep in Korean order (family name last in subtitles) unless the character uses a Western name themselves
- Stage names and nicknames → keep as-is
